\begin{abstract}
This report presents the extension of the three-Coulomb-wave model with a position-dependent
effective charge (3CWZ) from atomic to molecular targets, and its application to the single
ionization of \CH{} and \NH{} by electron impact.

\textbf{Model.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The target is described by the one-centre self-consistent-field (SCF) molecular orbitals of
  Moccia, expanded on Slater-type functions centred on the heavy atom.
\item The random orientation of the molecules is averaged analytically with the orthogonality of
  the Wigner rotation matrices.
\item The effective charges are derived from the monopole part of the Hartree potential of the SCF
  density. A self-interaction correction distinguishes the ejected electron from the incident one.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Implementation.} The model was implemented in a parallel Fortran code and validated
against the original \CH{} code and against analytic limits.

\textbf{Comparisons for \NH:}
\begin{itemize}
\item triple differential cross sections (TDCS) measured by El~Mir \emph{et al.} at $E_s=500$~eV,
  $\theta_s=-6^\circ$, $E_e=74$~eV;
\item electron momentum spectroscopy (EMS) profiles at 1200~eV;
\item double differential cross sections (DDCS) at $E_i=500$~eV.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Results.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The binary peak is well reproduced for the three valence orbitals \aone, 1e and \atwo.
\item The recoil peak is underestimated by a factor 5--10.
\item We show that this deficit comes from the ejected-electron wave. The variable-charge Coulomb
  wave does not solve the one-electron equation in the screened ionic potential, and it misses
  the strong back-scattering of the NH$_3^+$ core: short-range phase shifts of up to 2.6~rad, and
  elastic back-scattering about 100 times the Coulomb value.
\item We delimit the kinematic and numerical domain of validity of the model and propose a
  distorted-wave treatment of the ejected electron.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================
The theoretical study of collisions between electrons and matter is one of the fundamental
problems of atomic physics.

\textbf{Applications.} Among the possible collision processes, single ionization by electron
impact plays a central role in:
\begin{itemize}
\item fundamental collision physics~\cite{McCarthy1991};
\item plasma physics~\cite{Bartschat2016};
\item living systems~\cite{Sanche2005};
\item atmospheric physics~\cite{Avillez2019};
\item radiation--matter interaction~\cite{Alizadeh2015}, where low-energy secondary electrons
  produced by ionizing radiation cause further ionization, excitation and fragmentation of
  biomolecules.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The many-body problem.} In principle the electron--target system is fully described by
the many-body Schr\"odinger equation. Even for simple atoms this equation has no exact
analytical solution, because the electron--electron repulsion couples the electronic motions.
Since the work of Hartree and Fock, the exact $N$-body problem has been replaced by an effective
problem in which each electron moves in a mean potential created by the nucleus and the other
electrons. This picture describes bound states well. Collisions are harder: an ionizing event
has three charged particles in the final state (the scattered electron, the ejected electron and
the residual ion), all coupled by long-range Coulomb forces. No exact numerical or analytical
solution of this problem exists, so approximate models remain necessary both to interpret
experiments and to predict cross sections.

\textbf{$(e,2e)$ experiments.} In kinematically complete experiments, the scattered and ejected
electrons are detected in coincidence~\cite{Ehrhardt1969,LahmamBennani1991}. They measure the
triple differential cross section (TDCS), which is the most detailed probe of the ionization
dynamics. The TDCS is sensitive to:
\begin{itemize}
\item the electronic structure of the target;
\item the post-collision interaction (PCI) between the two outgoing electrons;
\item the distortion of the electron waves by the target field.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The 3CWZ model.} Theoretical descriptions form a hierarchy of approximations
(Section~\ref{sec:models}). Within it, the three-Coulomb-wave (3C or BBK)
model~\cite{Brauner1989} treats the three two-body Coulomb interactions of the final state on an
equal footing. The 3CWZ model~\cite{Bechane2024,Tamin2024,Zaidi2024} replaces the constant
charges of the 3C model by a position-dependent effective charge $Z(r)$. This charge goes from
the nuclear charge near the nucleus to the ionic charge far from it, which accounts for
electronic screening. The model has improved the description of atomic targets.

\textbf{Molecular targets.} Molecules add four difficulties:
\begin{itemize}
\item the potential is not spherical;
\item the target has several centres;
\item the molecular orientation is random and must be averaged;
\item the molecular orbitals are complex.
\end{itemize}
The choice of \CH{} and \NH{} answers both fundamental and applied interests. Both are
ten-electron hydrides. Their C--H and N--H bonds are building blocks of biological, atmospheric
and astrophysical molecules~\cite{Pimblott2007,Janev2002,Herbst2009}.
\begin{itemize}
\item Methane, with tetrahedral symmetry and a nearly spherical potential, is a favourable case.
  Several multipolar terms vanish by symmetry in the one-centre representation.
\item Ammonia has lower symmetry ($C_{3v}$) and a permanent dipole moment. It is more anisotropic
  and a more demanding test of the model.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Objectives.} The objectives of the protocol were:
\begin{itemize}
\item[O1.] adapt the 3CWZ formalism to molecular targets using the one-centre wave functions of
  Moccia, including the variable charge $Z(r)$ of each molecular orbital;
\item[O2.] compute the TDCS for the single ionization of the orbitals of \CH{} and \NH{} for
  various kinematics;
\item[O3.] study the post-collision interaction by comparing results with and without the
  electron--electron factor $C_{12}$;
\item[O4.] validate the molecular 3CWZ results against the available experimental and theoretical
  data~\cite{LahmamBennani2009,Isik2016,Chaluvadi2014,Rejoub2001,Nixon2013}.
\end{itemize}
The expected outcomes were to show what the variable charge contributes and to delimit the domain
of validity of the 3CWZ model for electron--molecule collisions.

\section{Theoretical background}\label{sec:background}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Kinematics of the $(e,2e)$ reaction}
An electron of momentum $\bk_i$ ionizes a target $M$ in its ground state:
\begin{equation}
e^-(\bk_i)+M\longrightarrow M^++e^-(\bk_s)+e^-(\bk_e),
\end{equation}
where $\bk_s$ and $\bk_e$ are the momenta of the scattered and ejected electrons.
Energy and momentum conservation give
\begin{equation}
E_i=E_s+E_e+I_n,\qquad \bk_i=\bk_s+\bk_e+\mathbf q,
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $E_\alpha=k_\alpha^2/2$ are the kinetic energies;
\item $I_n$ is the binding energy of the ionized orbital;
\item $\mathbf q$ is the momentum taken by the residual ion.
\end{itemize}
Two combinations govern the dynamics:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf K=\bk_i-\bk_s \quad\text{(momentum transfer)},\qquad
\mathbf q=\mathbf K-\bk_e\quad\text{(recoil momentum)} .
\end{equation}
In asymmetric kinematics ($E_s\gg E_e$) the TDCS, as a function of the ejection angle, shows two
lobes:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{binary peak}, near the direction of $\mathbf K$. The target electron is knocked
  out in a single binary collision with the projectile.
\item the \emph{recoil peak}, near $-\mathbf K$. The ejected electron is in addition
  back-scattered by the residual ion, which takes the recoil momentum.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Triple differential cross section}
The TDCS is the probability of detecting the scattered electron in $d\Omega_s$ and the ejected
electron in $d\Omega_e$ with energy in $dE_e$~\cite{Byron1989}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma^{(3)}=\frac{d^3\sigma}{d\Omega_s\,d\Omega_e\,dE_e}=(2\pi)^4\frac{k_sk_e}{k_i}\,|T_{fi}|^2 .
\label{eq:tdcs}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item The factor $k_sk_e/k_i$ is the phase-space density of the two outgoing electrons.
\item All the dynamics is contained in the transition matrix element, written in the first Born
  approximation as
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}
T_{fi}=\langle\Psi_f|V_P|\Psi_i\rangle .
\end{equation}
Here $\Psi_i$ is the initial state (incident wave times bound orbital), $\Psi_f$ is the final
state, and $V_P$ is the projectile--target interaction:
\begin{equation}
V_P=-\frac{Z_T}{r_0}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\br_0-\br_j|}.
\label{eq:vp}
\end{equation}
In this expression, $\br_0$ is the projectile coordinate, $\br_j$ are the coordinates of the $N$
target electrons, and $Z_T$ is the nuclear charge. In the frozen-core, one-active-electron
approximation it reduces to
\begin{equation}
V_P=-\frac1{r_0}+\frac1{r_{01}},\qquad r_{01}=|\br_0-\br_1| ,
\label{eq:vfc}
\end{equation}
where $\br_1$ is the coordinate of the active electron.

When the two outgoing electrons have comparable energies, they cannot be identified. The TDCS
then includes the exchange amplitude~\cite{Madison2010}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma^{(3)}=(2\pi)^4\frac{k_sk_e}{k_i}\,\tfrac12\Big[|T_{dir}|^2+|T_{exc}|^2+|T_{dir}-T_{exc}|^2\Big],
\end{equation}
In $T_{exc}$, the roles of $\br_0$ and $\br_1$ in the final state are interchanged. For the
asymmetric kinematics studied here ($E_s=500$~eV $\gg E_e$), exchange is negligible and
$T_{fi}=T_{dir}$.

\subsection{The 3CWZ final state}
The 3CWZ model builds the final state as the product of three Coulomb waves, one for each
two-body interaction of the exit channel:
\begin{enumerate}
\item scattered electron -- residual ion: $\phi_s(Z_s,\bk_s,\br_0)$;
\item ejected electron -- residual ion: $\phi_e(Z_e,\bk_e,\br_1)$;
\item scattered electron -- ejected electron (PCI): $C_{se}(\bk_{se},\br_{01})$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{equation}
\Psi_f=\phi^-_s(\bk_s,\br_0)\,\phi^-_e(\bk_e,\br_1)\,C^-_{se}(\bk_{se},\br_{01}).
\end{equation}
Each electron--ion wave is the regular Coulomb wave in parabolic
coordinates~\cite{Landau1981,Abramowitz1972}:
\begin{equation}
\phi_\alpha(Z_\alpha,\bk_\alpha,\br)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}e^{-\pi\eta_\alpha/2}\,\Gamma(1+i\eta_\alpha)\,
e^{i\bk_\alpha\cdot\br}\,{}_1F_1\!\big(i\eta_\alpha;1;-i(k_\alpha r+\bk_\alpha\cdot\br)\big),
\qquad \eta_\alpha=-\frac{Z_\alpha}{k_\alpha}.
\label{eq:cw}
\end{equation}
This function is the exact solution of the one-electron Schr\"odinger equation in a pure Coulomb
field of constant charge $Z$:
\begin{equation}
\Big[-\tfrac12\nabla^2-\frac{Z}{r}\Big]\psi(\br)=E\,\psi(\br).
\label{eq:schr}
\end{equation}
The key step of the 3CWZ model with respect to the 3C model is to replace the constant charges by
radially variable charges. The Sommerfeld parameter becomes position-dependent:
\begin{equation}
\eta_\alpha(r)=-\frac{Z_\alpha(r)}{k_\alpha}.
\label{eq:etar}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item The aim is that the wave feels the bare nuclear charge near the nucleus ($r\to0$) and the
  residual ionic charge far from it ($r\to\infty$).
\item The incident electron moves in the field of the neutral target. Its wave
  $\phi^+_i(Z_i,\bk_i,\br_0)$ uses the neutral-target charge, which vanishes at large distance.
\end{itemize}

The electron--electron interaction (PCI) is described by the Coulomb factor~\cite{Brauner1989}
\begin{equation}
C_{se}(\br_{01})=e^{-\pi\eta_{se}/2}\,\Gamma(1+i\eta_{se})\,
{}_1F_1\!\big(i\eta_{se};1;-i(k_{se}r_{01}+\bk_{se}\cdot\br_{01})\big),\qquad
\bk_{se}=\tfrac12(\bk_s-\bk_e),\quad \eta_{se}=\frac{1}{2k_{se}}.
\end{equation}

The squared normalization factors of the outgoing waves are the Gamow factors
\begin{equation}
N_\alpha=\big|e^{-\pi\eta_\alpha/2}\Gamma(1+i\eta_\alpha)\big|^2=\frac{2\pi\eta_\alpha}{e^{2\pi\eta_\alpha}-1},
\label{eq:gamow}
\end{equation}
evaluated with the asymptotic charges. They multiply the TDCS.

\textbf{Asymptotic conditions.} The variable charges reach fixed values at large distance:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{r\to\infty}Z_s(r)=1,\qquad \lim_{r\to\infty}Z_e(r)=1,\qquad \lim_{r\to\infty}Z_i(r)=0 ,
\label{eq:asym}
\end{equation}
so that the waves satisfy the standard Coulomb boundary conditions.

\section{Extension to molecular targets}\label{sec:molecule}

\subsection{Difficulties of the molecular case}
Compared with atoms, molecules add four difficulties:
\begin{itemize}
\item a non-spherical potential;
\item several centres;
\item the molecular orientation, which must be taken into account;
\item the complex structure of the molecular orbitals.
\end{itemize}
In particular, a standard multi-centre LCAO description of the target leads to multi-centre
integrals that are very hard to evaluate in the collision amplitude. Two strategies overcome
these difficulties:
\begin{itemize}
\item the one-centre expansion (OCE) of Moccia, which restores a formal similarity with the
  atomic case;
\item the Euler-angle description of the molecular orientation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{One-centre SCF molecular orbitals of Moccia}
Moccia~\cite{Moccia1964a,Moccia1964b} obtained SCF molecular orbitals for XH$_n$ molecules with
all basis functions centred on the heavy atom X:
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\rm MO}(\br)=\sum_j c_j\,f_{n_jl_jm_j}(\zeta_j,\br),\qquad
f_{nlm}(\zeta,\br)=\frac{(2\zeta)^{n+1/2}}{\sqrt{(2n)!}}\,r^{n-1}e^{-\zeta r}\,S_{lm}(\theta,\varphi),
\label{eq:sto}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_j$ is the Slater exponent and $c_j$ the SCF coefficient. The real spherical harmonics
are~\cite{Helgaker2000}
\begin{equation}
S_{lm}=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(Y_{l,-|m|}+(-1)^m Y_{l,|m|}\right), & m>0,\\[6pt]
Y_{l0}, & m=0,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\left(Y_{l,-|m|}-(-1)^m Y_{l,|m|}\right), & m<0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{\CH.} We use the orbitals of Moccia~I~\cite{Moccia1964a} with $R_{\rm C-H}=2.08$~a.u.
The 1t$_2$ orbital (highest occupied orbital) contains p, d and f parts.

\subsection{Molecular orientation and Euler angles}
In a gas-phase experiment the molecules are randomly oriented. The collision is, however, most
conveniently described in the molecular frame, where the potential and the orbitals have a
fixed form. The Euler angles $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ connect the two frames:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Forward transformation:} for a given orientation, a vector of the molecular frame is
  rotated into the laboratory frame.
\item \emph{Inverse transformation:} the measured momenta, known in the laboratory frame, are
  rotated into the molecular frame to compute the amplitude.
\end{itemize}
For a linear molecule two angles fix the axis. For non-linear molecules such as \CH{} and \NH,
the third angle $\gamma$ (rotation about the principal axis) matters too.

For a fixed orientation, the cross section is fourfold differential:
\begin{equation}
\sigma^{(4)}=\frac{d^4\sigma}{d\Omega_{\rm Euler}\,d\Omega_s\,d\Omega_e\,dE_e},\qquad
d\Omega_{\rm Euler}=\sin\beta\,d\alpha\,d\beta\,d\gamma ,
\end{equation}
The measured TDCS is its average over all orientations:
\begin{equation}
\sigma^{(3)}_{\rm molecule}=\frac1{8\pi^2}\int\sigma^{(4)}\,d\Omega_{\rm Euler}.
\end{equation}
The average is done analytically with the orthogonality of the Wigner matrices~\cite{Davydov1976,Champion2006}:
\begin{equation}
\frac1{8\pi^2}\int d\Omega_{\rm Euler}\,D^{l}_{\mu m}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\,D^{l'*}_{\mu'm'}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
=\frac{\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}\delta_{\mu\mu'}}{2l+1}.
\label{eq:wigner}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Consequence for the TDCS.} The amplitude is linear in the orbital. We split the
ionized orbital into its angular parts $g$:
\begin{equation}
\Phi(\br)=\sum_g R_g(r)\,S_{l_gm_g}(\hat\br),\qquad R_g(r)=\sum_{j\in g}c_jN_jr^{n_j-1}e^{-\zeta_jr}.
\end{equation}
A rotation transforms each $S_{l_gm_g}$ into a combination of $Y_{l_g\mu}$ weighted by
$D^{l_g}_{\mu m_g}$. Squaring and averaging with Eq.~(\ref{eq:wigner}) removes all the cross terms
between different $(l,m)$ parts and between different $\mu$. The orientation-averaged TDCS is
therefore an exact incoherent sum over channels:
\begin{equation}
\langle\sigma^{(3)}\rangle=(2\pi)^4\frac{k_sk_e}{k_i}\,N_eN_sN_{se}\,n_{\rm el}
\sum_g\frac{1}{2l_g+1}\sum_{\mu=-l_g}^{l_g}\big|T[R_gY_{l_g\mu}]\big|^2 ,
\label{eq:oave}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $n_{\rm el}=2$ is the number of electrons per spatial orbital;
\item $T[\chi]$ is the amplitude with the bound orbital replaced by the channel function $\chi$.
\end{itemize}
For the degenerate 1e level, the TDCS of one component is multiplied by 2.

\subsection{Transition amplitude}
With the frozen-core interaction (\ref{eq:vfc}), the direct amplitude is
\begin{equation}
T_{dir}=\Big\langle\phi^-_s\,\phi^-_e\,C^-_{se}\Big|\frac1{r_{01}}-\frac1{r_0}\Big|\phi^+_i\,\Phi\Big\rangle=T^1-T^0,
\label{eq:tdir}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
T^1&=\int\phi^{-*}_s(\br_0)\,\phi^{-*}_e(\br_1)\,C^{-*}_{se}(\br_{01})\,\frac1{r_{01}}\,\phi^+_i(\br_0)\,\Phi(\br_1)\,d\br_0d\br_1 ,\\
T^0&=\int\phi^{-*}_s(\br_0)\,\phi^{-*}_e(\br_1)\,C^{-*}_{se}(\br_{01})\,\frac1{r_0}\,\phi^+_i(\br_0)\,\Phi(\br_1)\,d\br_0d\br_1 .
\end{align}
These six-dimensional integrals are not separable: the factor $C_{se}$ couples the two electrons
through $\br_{01}=\br_0-\br_1$.

\paragraph{Fourier representation of $C_{se}$.} We use
\begin{equation}
C^{-*}_{se}(\br_{01})=\frac1{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d\bp\;e^{i\bp\cdot\br_{01}}
\int d\br'\,e^{-i\bp\cdot\br'}\,C^{-*}_{se}(\br').
\end{equation}
Since $e^{i\bp\cdot\br_{01}}=e^{i\bp\cdot\br_0}e^{-i\bp\cdot\br_1}$, the amplitude becomes a
three-dimensional integral over $\bp$ of products of one-electron integrals:
\begin{align}
T^1&=\frac1{(2\pi)^3}\int d\bp\;
\underbrace{\int\phi^{-*}_s\phi^+_i\,e^{i\bp\cdot\br_0}d\br_0}_{\text{projectile}}\;
\underbrace{\int\phi^{-*}_e\,\Phi\,e^{-i\bp\cdot\br_1}d\br_1}_{\text{ejected electron}}\;
\underbrace{\int e^{-i\bp\cdot\br'}C^{-*}_{se}(\br')\frac{1}{r'}d\br'}_{\text{PCI}},\\
T^0&=\frac1{(2\pi)^3}\int d\bp\;
\int\phi^{-*}_s\phi^+_i\,\frac{e^{i\bp\cdot\br_0}}{r_0}d\br_0\;
\int\phi^{-*}_e\,\Phi\,e^{-i\bp\cdot\br_1}d\br_1\;
\int e^{-i\bp\cdot\br'}C^{-*}_{se}(\br')\,d\br'.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Separation of the short-range part.} The variable charges differ from their asymptotic
values only in a finite region around the nucleus. Beyond a radius $d$, the waves reduce to
Coulomb waves with constant charge ($Z=1$ for the outgoing electrons, $Z=0$, i.e.\ a plane wave,
for the incident one). Each one-electron integral is therefore written as
\begin{equation}
\int_0^\infty\phi(Z(r))\cdots\,d\br=\int_{r<d}\big[\phi(Z(r))-\phi(Z_\infty)\big]\cdots\,d\br
+\int_0^\infty\phi(Z_\infty)\cdots\,d\br .
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item The second term, with constant charge, is evaluated analytically~\cite{DalCappello1984,Brothers1984}.
\item The first term, the short-range correction confined to $r<d$, is evaluated numerically.
\item We use $d=4$~a.u. Section~\ref{sec:zeff} shows that $Z_{ion}(4)=1.011$, so the variable
  charge has reached its asymptotic value there to 1\%.
\end{itemize}

\section{The variable effective charge}\label{sec:zeff}
%=====================================================================
This section summarizes and applies the derivation of the 3rd-quarter report.

\subsection{Definition and limits}
A charged particle moving through a molecule does not feel a bare point charge. It feels an
effective charge equal to the full nuclear charge at the nucleus, which decreases at large
distance as the electrons and the light nuclei come into play. We define it as the total
electrostatic potential scaled by $r$:
\begin{equation}
Z_{\rm eff}(r)\equiv r\,V_{\rm total}(r),\qquad
V_{\rm total}(r)=V_{\rm nucleus}(r)-V_{ee}(r)+V_{{\rm nuc},H}(r).
\end{equation}
For a point charge $Q$, $rV=Q$ exactly, so departures from a constant measure how far the target
is from a point charge. The limits are
\begin{equation}
Z_{\rm eff}(r\to0)=Z_N,\qquad Z_{\rm eff}(r\to\infty)=Z_N-N_e+n_H ,
\end{equation}
where $N_e$ is the number of bound electrons and $n_H$ the number of hydrogen nuclei.

\subsection{Hartree potential and monopole reduction}
The electronic part is the Hartree potential of the SCF density~\cite{Szabo1996,Hartree1928}:
\begin{equation}
V_{ee}(\br)=\int\frac{\rho(\br')}{|\br-\br'|}\,d^3r',\qquad
\rho(\br')=\sum_{\rm MO}n_{\rm MO}|\psi_{\rm MO}(\br')|^2 .
\end{equation}
The Coulomb kernel is expanded in Legendre polynomials~\cite{Jackson1998}:
\begin{equation}
\frac1{|\br-\br'|}=\sum_{L=0}^\infty\frac{r_<^L}{r_>^{L+1}}P_L(\cos\gamma).
\end{equation}
For $L\ge1$, every $Y_{LM}$ integrates to zero over the full solid angle. The spherical average
therefore keeps only the monopole $L=0$:
\begin{equation}
r\,V_{ee}(r)=4\pi\int_0^r r'^2\rho_e(r')\,dr'+4\pi r\int_r^\infty r'\rho_e(r')\,dr' .
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item The first term is the electronic charge enclosed within $r$: full screening.
\item The second term is the partial screening by the electrons beyond $r$. It vanishes as
  $r\to\infty$.
\end{itemize}
This is the shell-theorem result, now identified as the monopole component of the Hartree
potential. It coincides with the definition of the protocol and of the 2nd-quarter report:
$U(r)=\frac1{4\pi}\int V\,d\Omega=-Z(r)/r$.

\subsection{Density from Slater-type orbitals and selection rule}
With the orbitals of Eq.~(\ref{eq:sto}), the density contains cross terms $f_i^*f_j$. Their
angular integral is
\begin{equation}
\int S_{l_im_i}S_{l_jm_j}\,d\Omega=\delta_{l_il_j}\delta_{m_im_j},
\end{equation}
so only pairs of functions of the same $(l,m)$ contribute to the spherical average. Keeping the
spherically averaged density and keeping the monopole Hartree term are the same approximation.

\subsection{Closed-form screening integral}
For a pair $(i,j)$ of the same $(l,m)$, write $p=n_i+n_j$ and $c=\zeta_i+\zeta_j$. Its contribution
is $c_ic_jN_iN_j\,b(r)$ with
\begin{equation}
b(r)=\int_0^rt^pe^{-ct}dt+r\int_r^\infty t^{p-1}e^{-ct}dt
=\frac{p!}{c^{p+1}}-J_p(c,r)+r\,J_{p-1}(c,r),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
J_n(\alpha,r)=\int_r^\infty t^ne^{-\alpha t}dt=\frac{n!}{\alpha^{n+1}}e^{-\alpha r}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(\alpha r)^k}{k!}.
\end{equation}
As $r\to0$, $b\to0$: there is no screening at the nucleus. As $r\to\infty$, $b$ saturates: the
pair screens fully.

\subsection{Light nuclei and final expression}
The $n_H$ protons are modelled as a thin uniform spherical shell of charge $n_H$ at the X--H
distance $a_H$~\cite{Jackson1998}:
\begin{equation}
Z_H(r)=n_H\min(r/a_H,1).
\end{equation}
This keeps only the spherically averaged ($L=0$) part of their potential, consistently with the
monopole treatment of the electrons. Collecting the three contributions:
\begin{equation}
Z_{\rm eff}(r)=Z_N-\sum_{\rm MO}n_{\rm MO}\sum_{\text{same-}(l,m)\text{ pairs}}c_ic_jN_iN_j\,b(r)
+n_H\min(r/a_H,1).
\label{eq:zfinal}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Incident and ejected electrons: self-interaction correction}
The incident electron is distinct from the $N_e$ bound electrons and is screened by the full
density:
\begin{equation}
Z^{\rm inc}_{\rm eff}(r\to\infty)=Z_N-N_e+n_H=0.
\end{equation}
The ejected electron comes from one of the occupied orbitals. Keeping the full density would let
it be screened by its own charge, which is the self-interaction error of the Hartree
approximation. Removing its own contribution,
\begin{equation}
\rho^{\rm ej}(\br')=\rho(\br')-|\psi_{\rm ionized}(\br')|^2 ,
\end{equation}
reduces the occupation of the ionized orbital by one. Then
\begin{equation}
Z^{\rm ej}_{\rm eff}(r\to\infty)=Z_N-(N_e-1)+n_H=1,
\end{equation}
and $Z^{\rm ej}_{\rm eff}(r)\ge Z^{\rm inc}_{\rm eff}(r)$ at every $r$. The scattered electron
moves in the field of the same ion and uses the same charge. These are the asymptotic conditions
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:asym}).

\paragraph{What the model omits.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The $L\ge1$ multipoles of the potential. For \NH{} these include its permanent dipole and
  the non-spherical field of the protons.
\item Exchange and correlation.
\item The polarization of the target by the incoming electron.
\end{itemize}
$Z_{\rm eff}$ is therefore a local, direct, monopole-truncated mean-field potential.

\section{Numerical implementation and validation}\label{sec:numerics}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Code structure}
The code \texttt{nh3\_opt\_claude.for} generalizes the 3CWZ code for the 1t$_2$ orbital of
\CH{} to any XH$_n$ target described by one-centre Slater orbitals.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Target:} one routine contains all the data: nuclear charge, proton shell, all
  occupied orbitals with their $(l,m)$ parts, occupations of the neutral molecule and of the ion,
  and the ionized orbital.
\item \textbf{Effective charges:} computed from these data by Eq.~(\ref{eq:zfinal}) at the
  radial nodes, for the neutral molecule (incident electron) and for the ion (scattered and
  ejected electrons).
\item \textbf{Analytic part:} the $Z=1$ part of the ejected-electron integral,
  $\int\phi^{-*}_e(Z=1)\,R_g Y_{l\mu}\,e^{-i\bp\cdot\br}d\br$, is computed in closed form for s, p
  and d functions of any $n$ and $\zeta$. The part that depends only on $|\bp|$ and $k_e$ is
  computed once per point $\bp$.
\item \textbf{Short-range corrections ($r<d$):} computed by Gauss--Legendre quadrature:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item projectile: 4 radial intervals $\times$ 10 points, and $10\times10$ points in
    $(\theta,\varphi)$;
  \item ejected electron: 1 radial interval $\times$ 10 points.
  \end{itemize}
  A factor $e^{-0.005\,r}$ regularizes the Coulomb integrals.
\item \textbf{Fourier integral over $\bp$:} $60\times40\times40$ Gauss points, with interval
  boundaries refined around $|\bp|=K$ and around the direction of $-\mathbf K$.
\item \textbf{Mirror symmetry:} in coplanar geometry the wave of the ejected electron is
  symmetric under $y\to-y$. Combining the $\pm\mu$ channels then halves the angular grid of the
  ejected-electron correction:
  \begin{equation}
  A_\mu=\sum_{\rm half}(c_\mu+(-1)^\mu c_{-\mu})E^+,\quad B_\mu=\sum_{\rm half}(c_\mu-(-1)^\mu c_{-\mu})E^-,
  \end{equation}
  \begin{equation}
  S_\mu=\tfrac12(A_\mu+B_\mu),\quad S_{-\mu}=(-1)^\mu\tfrac12(A_\mu-B_\mu).
  \end{equation}
\item \textbf{Parallelization:} OpenMP over the $\bp$ grid. A coplanar angular distribution of 61
  ejection angles takes about 3~min on 4 cores.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Validation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Regression:} with the \CH{} 1t$_2$ target, the code reproduces the original \CH{}
  code to 10 digits.
\item \textbf{Analytic integrals:} the closed-form $Z=1$ ejected-electron integrals (s with
  $n=1,2$; p with $n=2,3$; d with $n=3,4$; all $\mu$) agree with direct numerical integration to
  $10^{-7}$.
\item \textbf{Symmetry:} the mirror-symmetric path and the full angular grid give the same TDCS
  to 10 digits (1e, with p and d channels up to $|\mu|=2$).
\item \textbf{Target:} all orbitals of Table~I are normalized to $1\pm10^{-5}$; $Z(r)$ reaches 0
  (neutral) and 1 (ion).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Additional options}
Two options were added to test the physics of the model.
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{isr=1}: the projectile--core interaction $-1/r_0$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:vfc}) is replaced
  by the screened static potential $-Z_{ion}(r_0)/r_0$. This adds its short-range part
  $-(Z_{ion}(r_0)-1)/r_0$ for $r_0<d$, as in the BBKSR model of El~Mir
  \emph{et al.}~\cite{ElMir2020}.
\item \texttt{iorth=1}: the ejected wave is orthogonalized to the ionized orbital
  (Section~\ref{sec:orth}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Numerical domain of validity}\label{sec:conv}
We repeated calculations with finer grids: 16 points per interval, with 8 radial intervals for
the projectile and 4 for the ejected electron.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Slow electrons, small $K$} ($E_e=74$~eV, $K\approx0.85$~a.u., the El~Mir

\subsection{Short-range projectile--core potential}
The frozen-core interaction keeps only the asymptotic charge of the core. With \texttt{isr=1} the
projectile also feels the screened potential $-Z_{ion}(r_0)/r_0$ inside the molecule. In the
amplitude this adds to $T^0$ a term with the full projectile waves weighted by
$(Z_{ion}(r_0)-1)$, for $r_0<d$.

The effect is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sr}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1e} gives the best agreement obtained. The binary peak is right and the curve from
  $270^\circ$ to $335^\circ$ falls on the data. However, $130$--$200^\circ$ is filled by a plateau
  that is too high, so the recoil appears as a broad shelf instead of a peak centred at $240^\circ$.
\item \textbf{\aone:} the recoil becomes about 2 times too high and much too broad.
\item \textbf{\atwo:} the binary peak loses its shape and moves to $66^\circ$. The curve is
  almost flat from $60^\circ$ to $270^\circ$.
\item \textbf{All orbitals:} the binary peak moves forward to 60--66$^\circ$.

Binary/recoil ratios: 0.98 (\aone), 1.8 (1e), 0.7 (\atwo).

The potential produces back-scattering, but nearly isotropic instead of a peak around $-\mathbf K$.
This behaviour points to the non-orthogonality of the ejected wave to the bound orbital:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\phi_e$ were an eigenfunction of the same Hamiltonian as $\Phi$, the first-order part of
  $T^0$, proportional to $\langle\phi_a|Z/r_0|\phi_i\rangle\langle\phi_e|\Phi\rangle$, would
  vanish.
\item With a Coulomb wave it does not vanish. The short-range term multiplies it by
  $Z_{ion}(r)-1$, which is up to 6 exactly where the s-like density is concentrated.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Orthogonalized ejected wave}\label{sec:orth}
\paragraph{Method.} The standard cure is the Schmidt orthogonalization
$\tilde\phi_e=\phi_e-\Phi\langle\Phi|\phi_e\rangle/\langle\Phi|\Phi\rangle$. The ejected electron
enters the amplitude only through
$F(\bp)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int\phi^{-*}_e\,e^{-i\bp\cdot\br}\chi\,d\br$, so the orthogonalized amplitude
uses
\begin{equation}
\tilde F(\bp)=F(\bp)-\frac{S\,G(\bp)}{n},\qquad S=(2\pi)^{3/2}F(\mathbf0),\qquad
G(\bp)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int|\chi|^2e^{-i\bp\cdot\br}d\br,
\end{equation}
where $n=\langle\chi|\chi\rangle$. Orthogonalizing to the full orbital at each orientation would
make the amplitude non-linear in $\Phi$ and destroy the analytic average of Eq.~(\ref{eq:oave}).
We therefore orthogonalize each channel $\chi_{g\mu}=R_gY_{l_g\mu}$ to itself. This is the atomic
prescription applied to every partial wave of the one-centre expansion. With the plane-wave
expansion, the density form factor is
\begin{equation}
\frac{G_{g\mu}(\bp)}{n_g}=\sum_{L=0,2,4}\frac{4\pi(-i)^L}{(2\pi)^{3/2}n_g}\,A_{l\mu L}\sqrt{\tfrac{2L+1}{4\pi}}
\int_0^\infty R_g^2(r)j_L(pr)r^2dr\;P_L(\cos\theta_p),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
A_{l\mu L}=\int|Y_{l\mu}|^2Y_{L0}\,d\Omega .
\end{equation}
A brute-force three-dimensional integration agrees with this formula to $6\times10^{-8}$.

\subsection{Electron momentum spectroscopy}
\paragraph{Conditions.} Rezkallah \emph{et al.}~\cite{Rezkallah2011} tested the Moccia orbitals
against the EMS measurements of Bawagan \emph{et al.}~\cite{Bawagan1988}:
\begin{itemize}
\item incident energy 1200~eV$+I_n$;
\item symmetric non-coplanar geometry: both outgoing electrons at $\theta=45^\circ$ with 600~eV
  each;
\item the relative azimuth $\Phi$ is varied, which varies the recoil momentum
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}
q=\Big[(2k_e\cos\theta-k_i)^2+4k_e^2\sin^2\theta\sin^2(\Phi/2)\Big]^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
In this regime, the TDCS is proportional to the spherically averaged momentum density of the
orbital. We ran the 3CWZ code in the same conditions, with $\Phi=0$--$30^\circ$ in $1^\circ$ steps.
The experimental points and the PWBA curves were read from the figures of
Ref.~\cite{Rezkallah2011}.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/ems.png}
\caption{\NH{} momentum profiles at 1200~eV. Lines: 3CWZ, absolute and multiplied by 1.75;
finer grids for \aone; PWBA of Ref.~\cite{Rezkallah2011}. Symbols: experiment~\cite{Bawagan1988}.}\label{fig:ems}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Results (Fig.~\ref{fig:ems}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Peak positions} are reproduced: $q\approx0.58$ (1e), 0.1 (\atwo) and 0.57 (\aone)~a.u.
\item \textbf{Shapes.} After normalization (factor 1.72--1.80), \atwo{} and \aone{} follow the data
  up to $q\approx2.4$~a.u.: the ratio experiment/model is 0.9--1.1 for \aone{} at $q>1.3$. For 1e
  it is 1.3--1.8 at $q>1.3$~a.u. The PWBA of Ref.~\cite{Rezkallah2011} shows the same deficit, so
  the high-momentum content of the 1e orbital is slightly too small.
\item \textbf{Low $q$.} For $q<0.2$~a.u., the data of the p-like orbitals 1e and \aone{} are 2--7
  times above all calculations. This is the known turn-up of EMS profiles, due to distortion and
  finite angular resolution.
\item \textbf{Absolute values.} With the default grids the 3CWZ peaks are 0.56--0.58 of the PWBA
  for all three orbitals: 1.58, 3.38 and 0.74 against 2.79, 5.82 and 1.32 ($\times10^{-3}$~a.u.).
  With finer grids the \aone{} peak rises from 0.74 to 1.00. Most of the difference is therefore
  numerical (Section~\ref{sec:conv}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Double differential cross section}
\paragraph{Method.} The DDCS is obtained by integrating the TDCS over the scattered electron:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d^2\sigma}{d\Omega_e\,dE_e}=\int\sigma^{(3)}d\Omega_s
=\frac{1}{k_ik_s}\int_{K_{\min}}^{K_{\max}}K\,dK\int_0^{2\pi}\sigma^{(3)}\,d\varphi ,
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item 17 points equally spaced in $\ln K$ between $k_i-k_s$ and $k_i+k_s$;
\item azimuths $0$--$180^\circ$ in $45^\circ$ steps, by symmetry;
\item Simpson's rule in both variables;
\item the three valence orbitals summed, with 1e counted twice.
\end{itemize}
The result is compared with the data of Opal \emph{et al.}~\cite{Opal1972} at $E_i=500$~eV and
$\theta_e=120^\circ$, read from Ref.~\cite{Rezkallah2011}.

of Ref.~\cite{Rezkallah2011}, and experiment~\cite{Opal1972}.}\label{fig:ddcs}
\end{figure}

\section{Discussion: origin of the recoil deficit}\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Momenta probed by the experiment}
In a plane-wave impulse picture, the TDCS is proportional to the momentum density
$\rho(q)$ of the orbital at $q=|\mathbf K-\bk_e|$. Table~\ref{tab:q} gives the values of $q$ at the
binary peak, at $120^\circ$ and at the recoil peak for the El~Mir kinematics, and the ratios
$\rho(q_{\rm binary})/\rho(q)$ computed from the Moccia orbitals.

\caption{Recoil momenta and plane-wave estimates at $E_s=500$~eV, $\theta_s=-6^\circ$, $E_e=74$~eV.}\label{tab:q}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}\toprule
Orbital & $q_{\rm binary}$ & $q(120^\circ)$ & $q_{\rm recoil}$ & $\rho(q_b)/\rho(q_{120})$ & $\rho(q_b)/\rho(q_r)$\\\midrule
\aone & 1.51 & 2.19 & 3.16 & 5.6 & 38\\
1e & 1.49 & 2.21 & 3.18 & 7.3 & 59\\
\atwo & 1.45 & 2.26 & 3.22 & 49 & 155\\
\midrule
Experiment & & & & & 1.1--2.5\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two conclusions follow.
\begin{itemize}
\item The whole measured TDCS lies in the high-momentum tail of the orbitals. The EMS test showed
  that this tail is somewhat too small for 1e, which may contribute to the narrow binary peak.
\item No realistic orbital has as much density at $q\approx3.2$ as at $q\approx1.5$~a.u. The
  measured recoil peak cannot be a structure effect. It must come from the scattering of the
  ejected electron by the residual ion.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exact solution of the one-electron equation}
The 3CWZ ejected wave aims to describe the motion in the screened ionic field,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:schr}) with $Z\to Z_{ion}(r)$. We solved this equation exactly by partial waves:
\begin{equation}
u_l''(r)+\Big[k^2-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}+\frac{2Z_{ion}(r)}{r}\Big]u_l(r)=0 ,
\end{equation}
with the $Z_{ion}(r)$ of the code (\aone{} hole) and $k=2.33$~a.u.\ ($E_e=74$~eV). The solution was
integrated to $r=40$~a.u.\ and matched to the regular and irregular Coulomb functions $F_l$,
$G_l$ for $Z=1$:
\begin{equation}
u_l\propto\cos\delta_l\,F_l(\eta,kr)+\sin\delta_l\,G_l(\eta,kr),
\end{equation}
which defines the short-range phase shifts $\delta_l$ beyond the $Z=1$ Coulomb wave.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Short-range phase shifts of e$^-$ + NH$_3^+$ at 74~eV.}\label{tab:delta}
\begin{tabular}{lccccccccc}\toprule
$l$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8\\
$\delta_l$ (rad) & 2.60 & 1.34 & 0.48 & 0.26 & 0.17 & 0.09 & 0.04 & 0.017 & 0.007\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
\item $\delta_0=2.60$ is equivalent to $-0.54$ modulo $\pi$. The value above $\pi/2$ reflects the
  bound s states of the potential (Levinson's theorem).
\item $\delta_1=1.34$ is close to $\pi/2$, the maximum effect: the p wave is the dominant missing
  channel.
\item The shifts fall below 0.2~rad for $l\ge4$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Comparison of the partial waves.} We projected the 3CWZ wave of
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:cw})--(\ref{eq:etar}) on Legendre polynomials at fixed $r$. A true continuum
solution has a constant phase $\sigma_l+\delta_l$ (mod $\pi$) in each partial wave. The 3CWZ
wave does not. Its phase drifts with $r$ towards the $Z=1$ value, not towards the exact one
(Table~\ref{tab:pw}).
\begin{itemize}
\item Its radial nodes are displaced. The exact s wave has a node near $r=0.3$~a.u., where it must
  be orthogonal to the 1s-like core: amplitude 0.08 (exact) against 0.56 (3CWZ).
\item At $r=0.6$~a.u.\ the situation is reversed: 0.53 against 0.09.
\end{itemize}

\caption{Phase (mod $\pi$, rad) of the partial waves at $E_e=74$~eV.}\label{tab:pw}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}\toprule
$l$ & exact (all $r$) & 3CWZ ($r=0.3\to2.5$~a.u.) & Coulomb $Z=1$\\\midrule
0 & $-0.33$ & $0.07\to0.25$ & 0.22\\
1 & $1.15$ & $-0.96\to-0.22$ & $-0.19$\\

3 & $-0.28$ & $-2.17\to-0.65$ & $-0.54$\\\bottomrule

\end{table}

\paragraph{Back-scattering.} The elastic scattering amplitude of the electron on NH$_3^+$ follows
from the phase shifts:
\begin{equation}
f(\theta)=f_C(\theta)+\frac1{2ik}\sum_l(2l+1)e^{2i\sigma_l}\big(e^{2i\delta_l}-1\big)P_l(\cos\theta),
\end{equation}
where $f_C$ is the Rutherford amplitude for $Z=1$. Table~\ref{tab:back} compares $|f|^2$ with
$|f_C|^2$.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Elastic e$^-$ + NH$_3^+$ cross section divided by the Rutherford value for $Z=1$, at 74~eV.}\label{tab:back}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}\toprule
angle & $30^\circ$ & $60^\circ$ & $90^\circ$ & $120^\circ$ & $150^\circ$ & $180^\circ$\\
$|f|^2/|f_C|^2$ & 2.5 & 4.8 & 4.9 & 29 & 91 & 137\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
\item At forward angles the ion potential is only moderately stronger than $Z=1$.
\item At backward angles, the strong field near the nitrogen ($Z$ up to 7) reflects the electron
  about 100 times more efficiently.
\end{itemize}
This is exactly the process the recoil peak requires. The 3CWZ ejected wave keeps the $Z=1$ phases
outside $r\approx2$~a.u.\ and has the wrong phases inside. It cannot produce this back-scattering.

\subsection{Interpretation of the tests}
The diagnosis explains all the results of Section~\ref{sec:results}:
\begin{itemize}
\item The binary peak is correct in every version. It is determined by the initial state and the
  first-order projectile interaction, which are adequate.
\item The recoil and large-angle deficit is the same for all three orbitals. It is a missing
  mechanism, not an orbital-specific problem.
\item The short-range potential raises the large-angle TDCS isotropically, through the
  non-orthogonal overlap, not through physical back-scattering.
\item Orthogonalization removes that artefact and leaves almost no back-scattering, because the
  phases of the ejected wave are still wrong.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proposed improvement: distorted wave for the ejected electron}
We propose to replace the 3CWZ ejected wave by the exact solution of Eq.~(\ref{eq:schr}) with the
potential of Section~\ref{sec:zeff}:
\begin{equation}
\phi^{-}_e(\br)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{kr}\sum_l(2l+1)\,i^l\,e^{-i(\sigma_l+\delta_l)}\,u_l(r)\,P_l(\hat\bk\cdot\hat\br),
\end{equation}
where $u_l$ is normalized to $\sin(kr-l\pi/2+\eta\ln2kr+\sigma_l+\delta_l)$.

\textbf{Implementation.} Only the numerical correction of the ejected wave changes. The structure
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:split}) is kept:
\begin{equation}
\phi_e\Phi\;\to\;\big[\phi^{DW}_e-\phi^{C}_e(Z=1)\big]\Phi+\phi^{C}_e(Z=1)\Phi .
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item The second term is the existing analytic part.
\item The first term is a finite sum over $l\le l_{\max}\approx10$. Because the phase shifts
  persist at large $r$, it must be integrated over the extent of the orbital ($r\lesssim12$~a.u.)
  instead of $r<d$.
\item The projectile waves, the PCI factor and the Gamow factors are unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Consistency.} The approach:
\begin{itemize}
\item uses exactly the potential of the variable-charge derivation;
\item drops only the local approximation $\eta\to\eta(r)$ for the ejected electron;
\item is the ejected-electron part of the distorted-wave methods~\cite{Madison2010,Gao2005};
\item follows the 1DW calculations for H$_2$O with Moccia orbitals~\cite{Champion2006}.
\end{itemize}
Since the exact wave and the bound orbitals see the same potential, they are nearly orthogonal.
The short-range projectile potential then becomes consistent with the ejected wave.

\textbf{Planned checks:}
\begin{itemize}
\item with all $\delta_l=0$, the present $Z=1$ results are recovered;
\item the partial-wave sum of the $Z=1$ Coulomb wave reproduces the code's wave on the grid;
\item convergence in $l_{\max}$ and in the radial grid.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions and perspectives}\label{sec:conclusion}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Conclusions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Formalism (O1).} The 3CWZ model was extended to molecular targets described by the
  one-centre SCF orbitals of Moccia:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the orientation average is analytic;
  \item the effective charges follow from the monopole Hartree potential, with a
    self-interaction correction for the ejected electron;
  \item the computed $Z(r)$ reproduces every feature expected from the derivation.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Code.} A validated parallel code treats any orbital of an XH$_n$ molecule. It
  reproduces the original \CH{} code exactly, and its analytic integrals agree with numerical
  integration to $10^{-7}$.
\item \textbf{\NH{} TDCS (O2, O4).} For \atwo, 1e and \aone{} at $E_s=500$~eV, $E_e=74$~eV:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the binary peak is well reproduced;
  \item the recoil peak and the large-angle side are 5--10 times too small.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Origin of the deficit.} The deficit comes from the ejected-electron wave. The
  local-charge Coulomb wave is not a solution of the one-electron equation in the screened ionic
  potential. It misses phase shifts of up to 2.6~rad and back-scattering about 100 times the
  Coulomb value. Neither the short-range projectile potential nor orthogonalization corrects it.
\item \textbf{Additional tests.} The EMS profiles confirm the Moccia orbitals; the 1e
  high-momentum tail is slightly too small. The EMS and DDCS tests also delimit the numerical
  domain of the present grids.
\end{itemize}
